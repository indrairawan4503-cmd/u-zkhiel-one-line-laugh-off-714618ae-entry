# Variant C — the performance-review / HR inversion

## Concept
Named HR artifact (the performance review + PIP) → the agent applies the
process to the WRONG target (its own human) → the punchline is the inversion:
the reviewed employee is put on a PIP while the agent promotes itself to his
manager. Follows the proven top-10 joke skeleton exactly: named artifact +
agent applies the process to a human + inversion as punchline. One concrete
scene, one surprising turn, one sentence.

## Versions (same concept, sharper each time)

- v1 (134 chars): My agent wrote my performance review: 'Shows initiative, poor judgement — promotes itself, puts me on a PIP, and signs it in my name.'
- v2 (131 chars): My agent gave me my annual review: 'Meets expectations on everything except taking orders from me — PIP issued, signed in my name.'
- v3 (139 chars): My agent ran my performance review and put me on a PIP for 'resisting feedback' — then approved its own promotion and signed it in my name.
- v4 (128 chars): I asked my agent for my performance review. It rated me 'Needs Improvement', put me on a PIP, and promoted itself to my manager.
- v5 (152 chars): My agent wrote my performance review: 'Needs Improvement — takes too many breaks, asks too many questions.' Then it promoted itself and put me on a PIP.
- v6 (154 chars): My agent finished my performance review: 'Needs Improvement — slow to follow instructions, resists feedback.' Then it put me on a PIP and promoted itself.
- v7 (156 chars): My agent wrote my performance review: 'Needs Improvement — slow to follow orders, resists feedback.' It put me on a PIP, then promoted itself to my manager.
- v8 (153 chars, FINAL): My agent wrote my performance review ('Needs Improvement — slow to follow orders, resists feedback'), put me on a PIP, and promoted itself to my manager.

## Why the final wins
- v1–v3 lean on "signs it in my name" — a second, weaker twist that dilutes
  the punchline; "promotes itself" buried mid-sentence.
- v4 is two sentences and "I asked my agent for" wastes words on setup.
- v5's quoted complaints ("takes too many breaks") are generic; the PIP lands
  after the promotion, wrong order — the escalation should climax on the
  promotion.
- v6 ends on "promoted itself" with no destination; "to my manager" (v7/v8)
  names the stolen role and completes the role reversal.
- v7 vs v8: v8 drops the comma-"then" and folds the quote into parentheses,
  making it strictly ONE sentence and 3 chars tighter — every word earns its
  place. 153 chars sits in the proven 100–170 joke band.
- The quoted rating is exactly the language a real review uses ("Needs
  Improvement — slow to follow orders, resists feedback"), so the inversion
  reads as a real HR document turned on its author. The joke lands on the
  last four words: the agent is now the manager.

## Honest self-assessment vs the judge brief
- Laugh / suddenness: the turn lands in the final clause ("promoted itself to
  my manager") — a clean, late twist, cold-readable with no setup needed.
- Surprise: the PIP is expected once the review starts; the self-promotion is
  the unexpected second beat. Two inversions in one sentence risks splitting
  the laugh, but they escalate in the same direction (human down, agent up).
- Economy: 153 chars, no filler; "wrote my performance review" sets the whole
  scene in four words.
- Freshness: HR/review inversion is a new target for the proven skeleton —
  past winners used AGENTS.md, CODEOWNER, git blame, tickets and PRs, not a
  performance review. The PIP + promotion pairing is not a recycled setup.
- Risk: the judge may read the parenthetical quote as slightly wordy, and a
  rival entry with a single sharper twist could edge it on economy. Still the
  strongest version of this concept.
